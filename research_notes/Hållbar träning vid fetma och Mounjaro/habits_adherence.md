# Habit formation, exercise adherence and engagement-sustaining app features (for a 51-year-old woman with class III obesity, bipolar disorder, starting tirzepatide)

Evidence grading used below: **[MA]** meta-analysis / systematic review; **[RCT]** randomized trial (incl. micro-randomized trials, MRT); **[FIELD]** large field experiment / megastudy; **[OBS]** observational / longitudinal; **[LAB]** short lab/online experiment; **[POP]** popular book / practitioner claim without controlled evidence. Where a figure came only from a secondary summary (news, university press), it says so.

---

## 1. How long habits take, and how they form (Lally 2010, Singh 2024, gym data); cue consistency vs. flexible timing

### Takeaway
Habits form gradually along an asymptotic curve. Typical medians are about 2 months (59–66 days), with a very wide range (4–335 days). Gym and exercise habits take longer than simple behaviours: around 6 weeks at 4+ sessions/week at best, and 4–7 months in large gym datasets. The first two weeks, when she usually quits, fall well before any habit could have formed, so the app has to carry her through months 1–4. Single misses barely matter. What matters is how much time passes since the last session and how often she shows up overall.

### Cited Findings
- **Singh et al. 2024 (Healthcare 12(23):2488) [MA]:** 20 studies, 2,601 adults. Only 4 studies reported time-to-habit: medians 59–66 days, means 106–154 days, individual range 4–335 days. Pooled pre–post improvement in habit strength SMD 0.69 (95% CI 0.49–0.88). Only ~23% of participants reached the predefined habit-formation threshold. Timing data were narratively synthesised because of heterogeneity. The authors call the "21 days" claim unsupported. — [Singh et al. 2024 PDF](https://sochob.cl/web1/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Time-to-Form-a-Habit-A-Systematic-Review-and-Meta-Analysis-of-Health-Behaviour-Habit-Formation-and-Its-Determinants.pdf); [Adelaide repository record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/01/250124151347.htm)
- **Lally et al. 2010 (Eur J Soc Psychol) [OBS, n=96, 39–~82 modelled]:** Participants picked one eating, drinking or activity behaviour in a daily context. Automaticity rose along an asymptotic curve. Time to reach 95% of the plateau was 18–254 days, median 66. Missing one opportunity "did not materially affect the habit formation process", but people who were very inconsistent did not form habits. Exercise behaviours took longer than eating or drinking behaviours (from training knowledge of the paper, not re-verified in this session). — [UCL News 2009](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [Lally et al. 2010 full text (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/70653523.pdf)
- A secondary source reports the automaticity drop from one missed day as about 0.29 points, described as "very small" (secondary, unverified). — [Inverse](https://inverse.com/health/62104-how-to-stick-to-new-years-resolutions)
- A registered-report multi-site replication preprint notes the Lally findings were based on a small sample and had not been replicated. It suggests lower consistency may compromise habit formation (snippet only; result not confirmed). — [PCI RR preprint](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/download/t_recommendations.reply_pdf.8382af6b73bdd0f7.6d616e757363726970745f7072657072696e742e706466.pdf)
- **Kaushal & Rhodes 2015 (J Behav Med) [OBS, n=111 new gym members, 12 weeks]:** About ≥4 exercise bouts/week for ~6 weeks was the minimum "dose" associated with an exercise habit. The authors advise keeping sessions enjoyable and simple and focusing on consistency. — [PubMed 25851609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)
- **Buyalskaya, Milkman, Duckworth, Camerer et al. 2023 (PNAS) [OBS + machine learning, >30,000 gym-goers, 4 years; plus hospital handwashing]:** Gym habits took months (reported as ~6 months on average, or 4–7 months), versus weeks for handwashing. Time since the last visit was the most important predictor of attendance for 76% of gym-goers. Day-of-week regularity mattered for 69%. Time of day mattered little. More habitual (predictable) people responded less to a later reward intervention. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/04/230417155750.htm); [HEC Paris summary](https://www.hec.edu/en/knowledge/articles/what-machine-learning-can-teach-us-about-habit-formation)

### Inferences
- "Time since last session" is the strongest predictor of return. The app's most important job after a miss is therefore to shorten the gap: prompt a tiny comeback within 24–48 h, without shame. This matches the megastudy finding in section 3.
- Time of day mattered little for gym habits, while day of week and recency mattered. That supports **flexible timing within a day anchored to a cue/routine** (e.g., "after morning coffee" or "when I put on my headphones") rather than a fixed clock time. This suits a person at home all day with variable energy.
- Set expectations explicitly: "This takes 2–4 months of practice; it will feel effortful in weeks 1–8; that is normal." This counters the "it's not working / I'm not the type" interpretation at week 2.
- Kaushal & Rhodes' ~4 sessions/week is a useful frequency target for a habit-forming "minimum version", but the sessions can be very short (see section 2).

### Gaps
- No study found that directly compares fixed-time cues with flexible "anchor-event" cues for people whose energy fluctuates (e.g., mood disorders, chronic fatigue). Recommendations here extrapolate from general-population data.
- No habit-formation timing data were found specifically for people with class III obesity or bipolar disorder.

## 2. Implementation intentions, habit stacking, Tiny Habits, minimum viable dose, identity-based habits: evidence strength

### Takeaway
Implementation intentions (if-then plans) have the best evidence, with small-to-medium meta-analytic effects on physical activity that are larger when they include barrier planning ("if I'm too tired, then I'll…"). Habit stacking is in effect an implementation intention anchored to an existing routine, and rests on the same context-cue habit theory. Tiny Habits (Fogg) and identity-based habits (Clear) are mostly popular-book frameworks without RCTs on physical activity. They are plausible and low-risk, but should not be presented as proven.

### Cited Findings
- **Bélanger-Gravel, Godin & Amireault 2013 (Health Psychol Rev 7(1):23–54) [MA, 26 studies]:** Implementation intentions increased physical activity, SMD 0.31 (95% CI 0.11–0.51) post-intervention and 0.24 (0.13–0.35) at follow-up. Effects were larger in clinical samples and when barrier management (coping plans) was included. Follow-up length did not significantly moderate the effect. — [McMaster Optimal Aging summary](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/HE/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322)
- **2018 meta-analysis of RCTs on implementation intentions for PA in adults:** pooled effect ~0.25 (reported via search summary; check the paper). — [PMC6235272](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6235272/)
- **Tiny Habits:** No published RCT of Fogg's method for physical activity was found. Fogg's own program data are self-report polls (e.g., 91% reported more confidence in creating habits), which is POP-level evidence. The closest RCT was a "Tiny Habits for gratitude" study (Hollingsworth & Redden 2022, n=154). — [Frontiers in Public Health 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.866992/pdf); [Coglode summary of Fogg](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/tiny-habits)
- A 2025 HCI paper explores 1-minute "micro-health interventions" as a gateway to habits (design research, not efficacy evidence). — [arXiv 2508.09312](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09312v1)
- **Kaushal & Rhodes 2015 [OBS]:** The habit threshold was defined by frequency (≥4/week), not session length, which indirectly supports "frequent and small" over "rare and long" for habit formation. — [PubMed 25851609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)

### Inferences
- An if-then plan plus a coping plan is the strongest evidence-based planning feature. For example: "När jag har druckit morgonkaffet, då sätter jag på en låt och dansar" ("When I've finished my morning coffee, I put on a song and dance"), plus a coping plan: "Om jag är för trött, då gör jag 1 låt sittande / går en sväng i lägenheten" ("If I'm too tired, I do one song seated / walk a lap of the flat").
- A "smallest version counts" rule follows logically from habit theory: repetition in context builds automaticity, and intensity does not. It also directly targets her "too cumbersome" and "all-or-nothing" reasons. The evidence is indirect (theory + Kaushal frequency data + Lally), not RCT evidence for tiny doses specifically.
- Identity framing ("Jag är någon som rör på mig varje dag", "I'm someone who moves every day") is low-cost and can be offered as an optional reflection, but should not be presented as evidence-based.

### Gaps
- No RCT evidence was found for Fogg's Tiny Habits or for "habit stacking" as branded techniques in physical activity. No experimental evidence was found for identity-based habits (James Clear, *Atomic Habits*; POP). Exercise-identity research exists but was not reviewed here (likely correlational).
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 2006 general implementation-intention meta-analysis (d≈0.65 across goals) is from training knowledge and was not re-verified in this session.

## 3. Lapses, the abstinence violation / "what-the-hell" effect, self-compassion, "never miss twice", and how misses affect habit strength

### Takeaway
A single miss does almost nothing to habit strength (Lally). The damage comes from how the lapse is interpreted (all-or-nothing) and from the gap growing. Self-compassion correlates with health behaviours and, experimentally, reduced post-lapse disinhibition in eating. The largest field experiment on gym attendance found that the best intervention was a small reward for **returning after a missed workout**. "Never miss twice" is not a tested slogan, but it is consistent with these data.

### Cited Findings
- **Lally 2010:** one missed opportunity did not materially affect habit formation; consistent inconsistency did. — [UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- **Milkman et al. 2021 (Nature) megastudy [FIELD, 54 four-week programs, 61,293 members of 24 Hour Fitness]:** The top intervention gave **micro-rewards for returning to the gym after a missed workout** (+27% weekly visits; reported as roughly 9 cents extra in one secondary account). 45% of interventions increased visits by 9–27% during the intervention, but only 8% produced significant effects after the 4 weeks ended. Expert forecasters could not predict which interventions would work. — [Mathematica summary](https://mathematica.org/publications/megastudies-improve-the-impact-of-applied-behavioural-science); [Scimex](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/which-programs-give-gym-goers-the-biggest-boost); [WEF summary incl. Milkman quote](https://www.weforum.org/stories/health-and-healthcare-systems/science-fitness-goal-motivation/)
- **Sirois, Kitner & Hirsch 2015 (Health Psychol 34(6):661–669) [MA of correlational samples, 15 samples, N=3,252]:** self-compassion correlated with health-promoting behaviours (eating, exercise, sleep, stress management), average r = .25 and positive in all samples, partly mediated by more positive and less negative affect. — [PubMed 25243717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25243717/)
- **Adams & Leary 2007 (J Soc Clin Psychol 26(10):1120–1144) [LAB experiment, college women]:** after a diet-breaking preload, a self-compassion induction reduced distress and reduced subsequent eating among highly restrictive eaters. This is a direct experimental demonstration of blunting the "what-the-hell"/disinhibition response (the term itself is not used in the paper). — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823)
- **Silverman & Barasch 2023 (J Consumer Research) [LAB/online, 7 experiments, >4,000 people]:** see section 6. Highlighting a broken streak lowered persistence, while the opportunity to repair the streak reduced the harm. — [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)

### Inferences
- Her stated reason "all-or-nothing after one missed day" is a textbook abstinence-violation pattern. The design response is to (a) make a miss non-visible or neutral, (b) celebrate the comeback more than the streak, and (c) use self-compassionate copy at the moment of return. Example: "Välkommen tillbaka! En paus raderar ingenting – allt du gjort finns kvar." ("Welcome back! A break erases nothing – everything you've done is still here.")
- "Never miss twice" can be reframed gently as "comeback within 2 days". It should be a suggestion, not a rule she can "fail". With bipolar disorder, depressive phases may cause multi-day gaps that are illness-driven, and the app must never imply moral failure.
- Weekly goals (e.g., 4 of 7 days) tolerate misses better than daily chains, and match the Kaushal ≥4/week threshold.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests "never miss twice" specifically. The self-compassion evidence is correlational (Sirois) or from eating behaviour (Adams & Leary). No exercise-specific self-compassion intervention RCT was reviewed. Neff/Germer-style interventions exist but were not searched.

## 4. Temptation bundling and music during exercise

### Takeaway
Temptation bundling (an enjoyable thing available only during exercise) raised gym visits in the original 2014 study. The effect decayed over time, and a large 2020 replication found smaller effects. Music during exercise has meta-analytic support for more positive affect, lower perceived exertion and better performance. For a person who loves music and dance, "music only during movement" plus dance-based sessions is the best-supported route to enjoyment. Enjoyment and immediate reward in turn predict persistence.

### Cited Findings
- **Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014 (Management Science) [RCT, N=226]:** gym-only access to tempting audiobooks increased visits by 51% (full treatment) and 29% (intermediate) versus control at first. Effects declined over time (notably after a Thanksgiving break). 61% of participants were willing to pay for gym-only access afterwards (from training knowledge, not verified here). — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/TemptationBundling.pdf); [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/2014-01-16/research/guilty-pleasures-and-obtaining-goals)
- **Kirgios et al. 2020 (Organ Behav Hum Decis Process 161:20–35) [FIELD RCT, N=6,792]:** audiobook access plus encouragement to bundle raised weekly workouts. Explicitly teaching temptation bundling added only a modest increment over giving the audiobook alone. — [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:161:y:2020:i:s:p:20-35); [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/oid_papers/150)
- **Terry, Karageorghis et al. 2020 (Psychol Bull 146(2):91–117) [MA, 139 studies, multilevel]:** music had beneficial effects on affective valence (the largest effect; g≈0.48 according to a secondary summary, unverified), physical performance and perceived exertion, plus better oxygen efficiency. There was no significant effect on heart rate. Benefits were larger in exercise than in sport, and with faster tempo (≥120 bpm). — [USQ record](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q57x6/effects-of-music-in-exercise-and-sport-a-meta-analytic-review); [Medical Xpress summary](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2020-01-music-workout-boost.html)
- **Woolley & Fishbach 2017 (Pers Soc Psychol Bull 43(2):151–162) [experiments + field survey]:** immediate rewards (enjoyment during the activity) predicted persistence better than delayed goals. Gym-goers who chose exercises they enjoyed did more sets, and persistence in cardio was predicted by experienced immediate enjoyment. — [Cornell summary](https://business.cornell.edu/news/2018/05/21/the-power-of-intrinsic-rewards/); [Chicago Booth Review](https://chicagobooth.edu/review/to-lose-weight-have-fun)

### Inferences
- Bundle her favourite or new music (or a dedicated "rörelse-playlist", movement playlist) with movement only. One song ≈ 3–4 min is a natural "smallest version" unit, and "dansa till 1 låt" (dance to one song) is a concrete, joyful minimum.
- The decay seen in Milkman 2014 argues for rotating content (new playlists, themed weeks, "låt-önskningar" (song requests) from family) to combat boredom, one of her stated dropout reasons.
- Tempo ≥120 bpm suits dance and walking. For a 140-kg starter, lower-impact moves (seated or standing dance, marching) at that tempo are still in scope. Joint-loading safety belongs to other researchers' sections.
- Her long-term dream (running outdoors with headphones) is ideal as an autonomous "why" and a milestone ladder: dance at home, then walk outside with music, then walk-jog intervals. It should not be the starting point.

### Gaps
- No replication of temptation bundling focused specifically on home exercise, women in midlife or people with obesity was found. No study links music specifically to long-term (>3 months) adherence. The Terry meta-analysis addresses acute effects.

## 5. Non-scale victories / alternative progress markers; remembering one's "why" (SDT, autonomous motivation)

### Takeaway
Self-determination theory evidence (Teixeira 2012 review, 66 studies) consistently links autonomous motivation, especially intrinsic enjoyment, to long-term exercise adherence. Externally controlled or body-image motives are less reliable. Progress markers that she personally values and can see every week (energy, mood, sleep, stairs, songs danced, minutes outdoors) are likely better than scale-only feedback. On tirzepatide, though, scale change will probably be visible and can be shown as one positive marker.

### Cited Findings
- **Teixeira et al. 2012 (IJBNPA 9:78) [SR, 66 studies]:** consistent positive relation between autonomous motivation and exercise. Identified regulation (valuing outcomes) tended to predict initial adoption, and intrinsic motivation (enjoyment) predicted long-term adherence. Evidence was mixed for health/fitness and body-related motives and for introjected (guilt-driven) regulation. Most studies were non-experimental. — [PubMed 22726453](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22726453/)
- **Woolley & Fishbach 2017:** immediate enjoyment predicts persistence better than distant goals. — [Cornell summary](https://business.cornell.edu/news/2018/05/21/the-power-of-intrinsic-rewards/)
- **Movement–mood link in bipolar disorder (NIH IRP) [OBS]:** increases in movement were followed by decreases in depressed mood, an association much stronger in people with bipolar disorder than in comparison groups. — [NIH IRP](https://irp.nih.gov/accomplishments/wearable-technology-revolutionizes-symptom-monitoring-and-treatment-for)

### Inferences
- Use two-stage motivation design. First, an explicit, personally written "why" (identified regulation) shown at moments of doubt, which addresses "forgets why she started / feels it doesn't matter". Second, gradually shift the emphasis toward enjoyment (intrinsic), e.g., "Vilken låt kändes bäst idag?" ("Which song felt best today?").
- Use non-scale markers she chose herself: a 1-tap post-session mood/energy rating (before vs. after shows immediate benefit), "låtar dansade totalt" (total songs danced), "minuter utomhus" (minutes outdoors), stairs or walking distance, sleep, and clothes fit. A pre/post mood check gives "visible results" from day 1 rather than week 6.
- Avoid guilt-based copy (introjected regulation) such as "Du lovade dig själv…" ("You promised yourself…").

### Gaps
- No RCT was found that specifically tests non-scale-victory displays or "why" reminders against standard feedback. Values-affirmation research in health behaviour was not searched in depth this session. No data were found on how GLP-1-driven rapid weight loss interacts with exercise motivation (e.g., whether attributing results to the drug undermines the perceived value of exercise). That is an important open question.

## 6. Digital health engagement: what sustains vs. backfires (self-monitoring, feedback, reminders, streaks, gamification, notifications, JITAIs, dropout curves)

### Takeaway
Self-monitoring plus feedback are the most consistently effective BCTs in physical activity apps. Goal setting, prompts, rewards and social support are associated with engagement. Gamified apps show small-to-moderate effects. Notifications reliably produce short-term opening and walking (×3.5 app-opening odds; +24% steps in 30 min in HeartSteps), but the effect habituates within weeks and does not by itself prevent long-term disengagement. Streaks motivate while intact but demotivate when visibly broken. Dropout from chronic-disease apps averages around 43%.

### Cited Findings
- **Meyerowitz-Katz et al. 2020 (JMIR 22(9):e20283) [MA, 17 studies]:** pooled dropout 43% (95% CI 29–57) in app interventions for chronic disease: 40% in RCTs, 49% in observational studies, 32% for metabolic-disease apps. I² > 99%. (The preprint reported 47%.) — [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2020/9/e20283)
- **Self-monitoring and feedback:** a 2021 BJSM meta-analysis of 28 studies found small-to-moderate PA increases from apps/trackers with automated self-monitoring and feedback, which it identified as particularly effective BCTs (via search summary). A 2023 weight-management app meta-analysis (Int J Nurs Stud) found apps with reminders, self-reporting and a health coach performed better. — [Search summary sources: Frontiers Psychol 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1227443)
- **Engagement BCTs (Frontiers in Psychology 2023, SR of 28 studies):** six BCTs were repeatedly associated with engagement: goal setting, self-monitoring of behaviour, feedback on behaviour, prompts/cues, rewards and social support. — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1227443)
- **Gamification:** gamified smartphone apps show small-to-moderate PA effects (summary reports SMD ≈ 0.34 across 12 apps, moderate-quality evidence). Which features maintain behaviour change remains unknown. — [PEDro evidence summary](https://pedro.org.au/english/systematic-review-gamified-smartphone-apps). A 2016 review found gamified health apps used few BCTs. — [Edwards et al. 2016, BMJ Open (PMC5073629)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5073629/)
- **HeartSteps MRT (Klasnja et al. 2019, Ann Behav Med) [RCT/MRT, ~44 adults, ~6 weeks]:** a contextually tailored walking suggestion raised steps in the next 30 min by 24% (+59 steps). The effect was +107% (+271 steps) at study start and **declined over time** (habituation). Anti-sedentary suggestions had no detectable effect. In piloting, 2 suggestions/day was judged the right frequency to minimise burden and habituation; the trial then randomised at 5 decision points to deliver about 3/day on average. Suggestions were not sent if the user was already active. — [PubMed 30192907](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/); [PMC6401341](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341)
- **Bidargaddi et al. 2018 (JMIR mHealth uHealth) [MRT, n=1,255, 89 days]:** push notifications increased app engagement within 24 h. The effect was largest for mid-day notifications on weekends. — [PMC6293241](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6293241/)
- **Bell et al. 2023, Drink Less app (JMIR mHealth uHealth) [MRT, n=350, 30 days]:** a notification raised the probability of opening the app in the next hour 3.5-fold (95% CI 2.91–4.25), but there was **no difference in time to disengagement** between daily fixed, random or no notifications. The authors raise habituation to daily notifications as a possible mechanism. — [JMIR mHealth 2023 e38342](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)
- **JITAIs:** Hardeman et al. 2019 SR (14 PA JITAIs, 6 randomised) found mixed evidence, with no study powered to detect effects. A 2023 meta-analysis of health-behaviour JITAIs (g=0.77) used only pre–post designs without controls, so it is likely inflated. — [Hardeman 2019, PMC6448257](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6448257/)
- **Streaks (Silverman & Barasch 2023, J Consumer Research) [LAB/online, 7 experiments, >4,000]:** intact versus broken streaks drove continued engagement (~66% vs ~58% chose another strength exercise), even with identical actual behaviour. Highlighting a broken streak lowered persistence. The harm was larger when people felt personally responsible and smaller when they could **repair** the streak. — [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); critique of "streak creep": [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
- **Megastudy durability:** only 8% of 54 interventions had significant effects after the 4-week program ended. Most nudges stop working when they stop. — [Mathematica](https://mathematica.org/publications/megastudies-improve-the-impact-of-applied-behavioural-science)
- **Bipolar self-monitoring [RCT, n≈47–50, 6 weeks]:** people with bipolar disorder used a Fitbit plus a symptom app (energy and mood items). The trial tested whether weekly review of the data affected adherence to monitoring. — [PMC7085979 (via search)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7085979); a home-exercise RCT in bipolar disorder (Project MOVE Bipolar) is registered, with results pending. — [NCT07767032](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07767032)

### Inferences
- Plan for habituation. Notification effects are front-loaded, so keep volume low (≤1–2/day), vary content, and suppress notifications once the day's minimum is done. The aim of notifications is to bridge the 2–4 month habit window, not to replace habit.
- An engagement drop around week 2 is the norm across mHealth, not a personal failing. The app should expect it and have a planned "week 2–3 re-engagement" moment (new playlist unlock, first progress summary, a family message).
- Prefer **cumulative, non-resetting counters** (total active days, total songs, total minutes) and **weekly goals** over daily streaks. If any streak is shown, it needs automatic "freeze/repair" days, and broken streaks must never be highlighted.
- In bipolar disorder, sharp energy rises could lead the app to escalate goals during (hypo)manic phases, followed by a crash. Goal escalation should be slow and capped, and should not respond automatically to a few high-energy days. A daily 1-tap energy check-in (low/medium/high) can pick the day's suggested dose rather than raise the baseline.

### Gaps
- No study was found that identifies an empirically "optimal" notifications-per-day count. The HeartSteps team's pilot judgement (2/day) and the Drink Less null long-term effect are the best available anchors.
- No evidence was found on streak mechanics in people with mood disorders, or on mHealth engagement in class III obesity specifically.

## 7. Social support: supportive messages from family members

### Takeaway
Evidence that family-delivered messages increase physical activity is thin: small pilots show they increase *perceived* social support and may sustain it longer. Social support is one of the six BCTs associated with app engagement. Family messages are low-risk and plausible as an engagement and "why" reinforcer, but are unproven as a driver of activity.

### Cited Findings
- **Pilot RCT, low-income Latino adults with T2D (n=42; JMIR Diabetes 2017; NCT02850770):** three arms (control; phone messages; phone messages plus messages to a family member/friend with ways to support). Social support rose in both message arms at 6 weeks, but only the family-inclusive arm sustained it at 12 weeks. It was underpowered for activity outcomes. — [JMIR Diabetes 2017](https://diabetes.jmir.org/2017/1/e8/PDF); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02850770](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02850770)
- **Couples RCT (Berli & Scholz; n=121 overweight couples):** partner-involved text messages on action control, with accelerometer-measured PA at 6 months (dyadic, not specifically family-message, design). — [Frontiers Psychol 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.754488/pdf)
- **2026 pilot:** a family-inclusive wearable plus text intervention in low-income communities tested feasibility only. — [JMIR mHealth 2026](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e76991/PDF)
- Social support is among the BCTs associated with app engagement. — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1227443)

### Inferences
- Let family (e.g., children or partner) pre-record or write short encouraging messages and song suggestions. The app delivers them at strategic moments (after a comeback, at milestones, on low-energy days) rather than daily, which keeps them novel and avoids "nagging".
- Family messages should support autonomy ("Vi är stolta över dig oavsett", "We're proud of you no matter what") rather than monitor or pressure ("Har du tränat idag?", "Have you exercised today?"). Controlling support could undermine autonomous motivation per SDT (inference from Teixeira 2012).
- She should control what family can see (e.g., opt-in sharing of "I danced today", never misses).

### Gaps
- No adequately powered RCT was found showing family-delivered supportive messages increase PA in adults. No data were found on the optimal frequency of supporter messages.

## App design implications

### Takeaway
Design for months 1–4, not for weeks 1–2. Make the minimum dose tiny and fun (one song), count every bit of movement, make misses invisible and comebacks celebrated, keep notifications few and adaptive to energy, and anchor motivation in her own "why", her music, and how she feels right after moving.

### Cited Findings (basis for each requirement; see sections above for details)
- Habit timeline is 59–66 days median, gym 4–7 months — [Singh 2024](https://sochob.cl/web1/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Time-to-Form-a-Habit-A-Systematic-Review-and-Meta-Analysis-of-Health-Behaviour-Habit-Formation-and-Its-Determinants.pdf); [Buyalskaya 2023](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/04/230417155750.htm)
- ≥4 sessions/week for ~6 weeks as a habit threshold — [Kaushal & Rhodes 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)
- Rewarding return after a miss was the best of 54 interventions — [Milkman 2021](https://mathematica.org/publications/megastudies-improve-the-impact-of-applied-behavioural-science)
- Broken streaks hurt, repair helps — [Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)
- Notification effects habituate, ~2/day judged appropriate in pilots — [HeartSteps](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341); [Bell 2023](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)
- If-then plus coping plans give SMD 0.24–0.31 — [Bélanger-Gravel 2013](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/HE/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322)
- Music improves exercise affect — [Terry 2020](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q57x6/effects-of-music-in-exercise-and-sport-a-meta-analytic-review); immediate enjoyment predicts persistence — [Woolley & Fishbach 2017](https://business.cornell.edu/news/2018/05/21/the-power-of-intrinsic-rewards/)

### Inferences – concrete requirements (MUST / SHOULD / AVOID)

**A. Minimum dose and logging ("det minsta räknas", "the smallest counts")**
- MUST: The minimum that counts as an "active day" is **1 song (~3 min) or 2 minutes of any movement**, including seated dance, walking around the flat, or stretching. Logging takes **one tap** from the home screen (≤5 seconds, no forms).
- MUST: Offer three daily dose levels chosen by a 1-tap energy check-in. Low energy: 1 song / 2 min. Medium: 3 songs / ~10 min. High: 5–8 songs / 20–30 min. All three count equally as an "active day".
- SHOULD: Habit target of **4 active days per week** (Kaushal & Rhodes), shown as a weekly ring or "4 av 7" (4 of 7), not a daily chain. Days 5–7 are bonus.
- SHOULD: Do not auto-increase the baseline. Suggest a step up at most **once every 2 weeks**, only if ≥4 days/week were met for 2 consecutive weeks, and only with her accepting it. This guards against hypomanic over-commitment and the "all-in then crash" pattern.

**B. Misses, comebacks, self-compassion**
- MUST: **No daily streak counter that resets to 0.** Use cumulative, never-decreasing totals ("42 aktiva dagar", "180 låtar dansade", "42 active days", "180 songs danced") and weekly goals.
- MUST: Never show "missed", red Xs or "you broke your streak". Inactive days are simply blank.
- MUST: **Comeback celebration** after ≥2 days inactive: a warm "Välkommen tillbaka" screen ("Welcome back"), extra-visible confetti or badge, and optionally a family message. Make returning the most rewarded event in the app (Milkman 2021).
- SHOULD: "Comeback within 2 days" gentle nudge: if inactive for 2 days, send one friendly suggestion of the 1-song version. If still inactive, back off to **one message every 3–4 days**, never escalating. During reported low mood or energy, nudges become optional, e.g., "Vill du ha påminnelser just nu?" ("Do you want reminders right now?").
- MUST: Self-compassionate copy at every lapse point (no "should", no guilt). Example: "Kroppen och humöret går i vågor. Det du redan gjort finns kvar." ("Body and mood come in waves. What you've already done is still here.")
- SHOULD: Optional "pausläge" (pause mode) for illness or depressive phases. Weekly goals are suspended without penalty.

**C. Notifications**
- MUST: Default **1 notification/day** at her chosen anchor time, with an optional **max 2/day**. Never more than 2.
- MUST: **No reminder once that day's minimum is logged** (HeartSteps did not prompt when already active).
- SHOULD: Rotate notification content (song suggestions, her own "why", family quotes, small facts) to slow habituation. Never send the identical text two days running.
- SHOULD: Quiet hours (e.g., 21:00–09:00) and no "you haven't…" alerts. Expect notification effects to fade over 4–6 weeks; by then the habit cue (anchor routine) should carry more load.
- AVOID: guilt-laden, nagging or broken-streak notifications, and notification frequency tied to inactivity (more misses must not mean more nags).

**D. Planning (implementation intentions)**
- MUST: At onboarding (and revisable), she writes 1–2 if-then plans tied to existing routines rather than clock times ("När jag druckit förmiddagskaffet, då…", "When I've had my mid-morning coffee, then…") plus **one coping plan for low-energy days** ("Om jag är trött, då dansar jag 1 låt sittande", "If I'm tired, I dance one song seated").
- SHOULD: Show the if-then plan on the home screen and in notifications.

**E. Music and enjoyment (temptation bundling)**
- MUST: Music is the core. Each session = "spela en låt och rör dig" ("play a song and move"). Link to her own playlists (Spotify or YouTube links) and a dedicated "bara-när-jag-rör-mig" (only-when-I-move) playlist.
- SHOULD: Fresh content every 1–2 weeks to counter boredom and bundling decay: a new themed playlist ("80-tal", "Disco", "80s", "Disco"), a new dance move, song requests from family.
- SHOULD: Prefer songs ≥120 bpm for dance and walking, but allow any song she loves.
- SHOULD: After each session, ask a 1-tap "Hur kändes det?" ("How did it feel?") 😊/😐/☹, which builds the evidence of immediate reward.

**F. Progress she can see (non-scale)**
- MUST: A weekly summary every Sunday or Monday showing active days, songs danced, minutes, pre/post mood shift ("Du kände dig bättre efter 8 av 10 pass", "You felt better after 8 of 10 sessions"), and minutes outdoors.
- SHOULD: Milestone ladder toward the dream of running outdoors with headphones: dance at home, then 1 song walking outside, then a 10-min outdoor music walk, then a 20-min walk, then walk–jog intervals. Show the ladder with her position on it.
- SHOULD: Optional weight or waist display as just one marker among many, and opt-in (she may want to see tirzepatide progress, but it must not dominate).
- SHOULD: Day-30, 60 and 90 reflection prompts: "Vad kan du göra nu som du inte kunde förut?" ("What can you do now that you couldn't before?").

**G. Remembering the "why"**
- MUST: Onboarding captures her own "why" in her words (and optionally a photo or voice memo). Show it on a long-press or "Varför?" ("Why?") button, on the comeback screen, and in about 1 in 4 notifications.
- SHOULD: Set expectations on day 1: "Vanor tar ofta 2–4 månader. Vecka 2–3 är svårast – appen hjälper dig igenom den." ("Habits often take 2–4 months. Weeks 2–3 are the hardest – the app helps you through them.")
- SHOULD: A planned **day 10–14 "booster"** (her usual dropout point), such as a new playlist unlock, a first summary, or a family message.

**H. Family support**
- SHOULD: Family can add short messages and song suggestions to a queue. The app releases about **1–2 per week** at comebacks, milestones and low-energy days, not daily.
- MUST: She controls what family sees. Default: nothing shared. Optional: share "active days this week" only, never misses.
- AVOID: family "check-up" features that monitor her or pressure her.

**I. Gamification guardrails**
- OK: badges for cumulative totals, comebacks, new songs or moves tried, and first outdoor walk.
- AVOID: leaderboards, points tied to calories or weight, loss-framed mechanics, daily streaks without automatic repair, and anything that makes the app feel like a test she can fail.
- AVOID: features that require typing, long forms or setup on every use ("too cumbersome").

**J. Bipolar-specific cautions (inferences; coordinate with the clinical-safety researcher)**
- Do not interpret a burst of high activity as the new normal (cap auto-suggestions, as in A).
- An optional mood/energy log can double as a self-monitoring aid. Prolonged very low or very high activity could prompt a gentle "Hur mår du? Kanske värt att prata med din vårdkontakt" ("How are you? Maybe worth talking to your care contact"), with no diagnosis-like language.
- Movement–mood coupling appears strong in bipolar disorder (NIH observational data), which supports making "move a little on low days" the default minimal suggestion.

**Suggested numbers summary**
| Parameter | Suggested value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum that counts | 1 song (~3 min) or 2 min movement | Habit theory (repetition > intensity); "too cumbersome" barrier |
| Weekly habit target | 4 active days/week | Kaushal & Rhodes 2015 |
| Habit horizon communicated | 2–4 months (median ~66 days; exercise often longer) | Lally 2010; Singh 2024; Buyalskaya 2023 |
| Notifications | 1/day default, max 2/day, none after logging, quiet hours | HeartSteps pilot (2/day); Bell 2023 habituation |
| Comeback nudge | after 2 inactive days, then ≤1 per 3–4 days | Buyalskaya (recency); Milkman 2021 (return reward) |
| Goal step-up | max once per 2 weeks, user-accepted | Inference (bipolar / all-in pattern) |
| Content refresh | new playlist/move every 1–2 weeks | Bundling decay (Milkman 2014) |
| Family messages | 1–2/week at strategic moments | Inference; pilot data only |
| Booster | day 10–14 | Her personal dropout pattern; mHealth attrition |
| Logging effort | 1 tap, ≤5 s | Barrier reduction |

### Gaps
- These numbers are informed defaults, not trial-validated optima. No study defines an optimal notification count, minimum session length for habit formation, or streak policy for people with mood disorders or class III obesity. The app should log her usage (active days/week, notification-to-action rates) so the defaults can be tuned for her personally after 4–6 weeks.
